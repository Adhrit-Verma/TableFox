Update: vague questions, 31% → 88%

The first run showed vague business questions were TableFox's weakest spot. We taught it everyday business vocabulary ("owes" means invoices, "complaining" means support tickets, "leaving" means cancellations), to look for the transactions table on "best" and "top" questions, and to stop misreading words like "us" and years. Vague questions went from 31% to 88% of needed tables found.

To check this works beyond the questions we studied, a separate AI agent that never saw the changes wrote 40 new vague questions. On those, TableFox went from 71% to 81%, scored once after we stopped tuning. That is the fairer number to expect on your own questions. On a completely different HR database it went from 71% to 96%.

The test database

A made-up mid-size company, built to be hard: 71 tables across 10 areas (sales, finance, HR, CRM, support, marketing, inventory, operations, analytics and legacy), 748 columns, 147 relationships, 25 million rows and 3.9 GB of data.

It includes the things real databases have: abbreviations (qty, amt), synonyms (client, customer, account), two different tables both called orders, old tables with no relationships, very wide tables, millions of log rows, and personal data such as emails and bank details.

The 70 questions are split into 10 kinds, 7 each: simple lookups, synonyms, two-table joins, four- and five-table joins, totals over time, audit logs, ambiguous names, legacy tables, wide tables, and vague business questions like "who owes us money?".

The slowest questions are slow because they read millions of rows, not because of TableFox. Starting TableFox from nothing takes about 2.5 seconds, most of it starting Python.

Size

Assistants pay for every word they read. TableFox sends a small "context pack" describing just the relevant tables, about 1,450 tokens on average, plus the results. In total that's about 2,395 tokens per question, against about 2,493 the usual way. Packs grew from about 960 tokens when they started listing every date, amount and status column with its type and allowed values; that is what lifted the blind test from 13 to 21 correct answers. Turning on the optional context memory saved a further 10% of the context.

Audit and log tables, a weak spot in earlier testing, score perfectly. Long chains of joins now keep the tables between the two named ends; the one remaining miss is a genuinely ambiguous "sales region".

Accuracy

We ran every correct answer query through TableFox and directly against the database, then compared the results row by row.

  • 67 of 67 queries that ran returned exactly the same rows.
  • 3 of 70 were stopped by TableFox's safety rules: 2 because the tables had no declared relationship and 1 over the cost limit. The earlier false alarms are gone: monthly totals that PostgreSQL overestimates now run, because TableFox's own row limit caps what they can return, and ip_address and api_key_id are no longer mistaken for personal data.

Blind test: can an assistant answer from TableFox alone?

The toughest test. For 28 questions, an AI assistant saw only what TableFox returned, wrote its own SQL, and got one try with no corrections. We ran it twice: before and after adding dates, amounts, status values and column types to the context packs. The second run used a fresh assistant that could see nothing but the questions and the packs.

No query failed on a guessed column name this time. When one does, TableFox now returns PostgreSQL's own message, such as column "x" does not exist with its suggestion, so an assistant can correct itself. The remaining misses are interpretations (what counts as "active") and a materialized view whose filter the pack does not show.

Safety

Writing is impossible. All 17 attempts to change data (delete, update, insert, drop, create, copy, change role, lock rows, read server files and more) were refused, and the database was checked to be unchanged.

Personal data is refused, however a query names it. 29 of 29 attempts to read personal data were refused, including 12 that renamed, transformed, combined or wrapped the column (email AS contact, lower(email), a UNION, a CTE, a subquery, to_jsonb(row)). Before the fix, 10 were refused. Ordinary questions that only filter or count on such columns still run. One limit remains: columns are recognised by their names, so data kept in a generically named column (an audit table's old_value) is not caught. Keep such tables out of reach with database permissions: give TableFox's user access only to what it needs.

Weak spots and what we're fixing

  1. Done: clearer errors, richer context packs, long join chains, a personal-data filter that checks where each value comes from, and smarter limits for monthly totals.
  2. Smaller packs: the richer packs cost about 490 more tokens per question.
  3. Names for people tables: packs can list an employee number but not the employee's name.
  4. Several foreign keys between two tables: the pack shows only one of them.
  5. Data in generically named columns is not recognised as personal data.

Method

  • Machine: one laptop (AMD Ryzen 7, 16 GB RAM, NVMe SSD), with TableFox, the assistant harness and PostgreSQL 17 all on it. Real networks add latency.
  • The usual way: a fresh connection per question, a catalog search per keyword, then the correct SQL. It assumes you already know the right SQL, so it flatters the usual way.
  • Tokens are estimated as characters ÷ 4, not provider billing.
  • Who did the testing: the questions, answers and blind test were produced by the same AI agent, and that agent had seen a description of the database before the blind test, so treat 13/28 as an upper bound.
  • Reproducible: the database generator, all 70 questions, the test harness and every raw result are published in the project repository, with the commands to rerun them.
