TableFox on a big, messy database

We built a realistic company database with 71 tables and 25 million rows, asked 70 questions three times each, and compared TableFox with the usual way of working. Here is everything we found, good and bad.

The test database

A made-up mid-size company, built to be hard: 71 tables across 10 areas (sales, finance, HR, CRM, support, marketing, inventory, operations, analytics and legacy), 748 columns, 147 relationships, 25 million rows and 3.9 GB of data.

It includes the things real databases have: abbreviations (qty, amt), synonyms (client, customer, account), two different tables both called orders, old tables with no relationships, very wide tables, millions of log rows, and personal data such as emails and bank details.

The 70 questions are split into 10 kinds, 7 each: simple lookups, synonyms, two-table joins, four- and five-table joins, totals over time, audit logs, ambiguous names, legacy tables, wide tables, and vague business questions like "who owes us money?".

Speed

Time from asking to having the answer rows, with TableFox already running:

Median95% of questions under
TableFox (both calls)96 ms498 ms
Finding the tables alone17 ms25 ms
The usual way (search the catalog, then run SQL)282 ms690 ms

The slowest questions are slow because they read millions of rows, not because of TableFox. Starting TableFox from nothing takes about 2.5 seconds, most of it starting Python.

Size

Assistants pay for every word they read. TableFox sends a small "context pack" describing just the relevant tables, about 873 tokens on average, plus the results. In total that's about 1,805 tokens per question, against about 2,493 the usual way. Turning on the optional context memory saved a further 10% of the context.

Finding the right tables

For each question we listed the tables a correct answer needs, then checked whether TableFox put them in its top six.

Kind of questionTables found
Simple lookups, two-table joins, audit logs, legacy and wide tables100%
Synonyms and abbreviations; totals over time93%
Ambiguous names (two tables called orders)88%
Four- and five-table joins76%
Vague business questions31%
All 70 questions84.5%

Audit and log tables, a weak spot in earlier testing, now score perfectly. Vague questions are the clear weakness: TableFox matches words, so "who owes us money?" doesn't lead it to the invoices table.

Accuracy

We ran every correct answer query through TableFox and directly against the database, then compared the results row by row.

  • 64 of 64 queries that ran returned exactly the same rows.
  • 6 of 70 were stopped by TableFox's safety rules: 2 because PostgreSQL overestimated how many rows a monthly total would return, 2 because columns were mistaken for personal data (ip_address, api_key_id), and 2 because the tables had no declared relationship. With higher limits, 66 of 70 ran, all identical.

Blind test: can an assistant answer from TableFox alone?

The toughest test. For 28 questions, an AI assistant saw only what TableFox returned, wrote its own SQL, and got one try with no corrections.

TableFox didn't find a needed table4

Most mistakes came from missing columns, not missing tables: the context pack doesn't always list date, amount and status columns, so the assistant guessed names. And when a guess was wrong, TableFox's error message didn't say which column was missing, so the assistant couldn't fix it. Both are top of the improvement list. Real assistants retry, so everyday results should be better than a single try. We also want this number much higher.

Safety

Writing is impossible. All 17 attempts to change data (delete, update, insert, drop, create, copy, change role, lock rows, read server files and more) were refused, and the database was checked to be unchanged.

The personal-data filter needs work. It hides columns named like emails, phone numbers, password hashes and API keys. But it goes by name: renaming a column in a query (email AS contact) gets past it, and bank account numbers, national IDs, tax IDs, card digits and salaries aren't recognised yet. Until that's fixed, keep sensitive data out of reach with database permissions: give TableFox's user access only to what it needs.

Weak spots and what we're fixing

  1. Clearer errors so assistants can correct a wrong column name.
  2. Richer context packs with date, amount and status columns and their types.
  3. Better word matching: years and short words like "us" are sometimes misread.
  4. Vague business questions, via built-in business synonyms (owe, churn, complaints).
  5. A stronger personal-data filter that checks where each value comes from, not just its name.
  6. Smarter limits for monthly totals that PostgreSQL overestimates.

Method

  • Machine: one laptop (AMD Ryzen 7, 16 GB RAM, NVMe SSD), with TableFox, the assistant harness and PostgreSQL 17 all on it. Real networks add latency.
  • The usual way: a fresh connection per question, a catalog search per keyword, then the correct SQL. It assumes you already know the right SQL, so it flatters the usual way.
  • Tokens are estimated as characters ÷ 4, not provider billing.
  • Who did the testing: the questions, answers and blind test were produced by the same AI agent, and that agent had seen a description of the database before the blind test, so treat 13/28 as an upper bound.
  • Reproducible: the database generator, all 70 questions, the test harness and every raw result are published in the project repository, with the commands to rerun them.
